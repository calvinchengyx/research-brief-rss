[A1] Measuring and Mitigating (Over)reliance to Build Human-Compatible AI
Lujain Ibrahim*, Katherine M. Collins [...] Diyi Yang* [...] Umang Bhatt
Proceedings of the AAAI/ACM Conference on AI Ethics and Society · 11 October 2026
In short: Argues that measuring and mitigating user overreliance on LLMs is central, consolidating risks and proposing measurement directions and mitigations.
Large language models (LLMs) differ from previous technologies in functioning as collaborative "thought partners'' that can engage fluidly in natural language across a range of tasks and increasingly act as autonomous agents on users' behalf. As they come to shape consequential decisions from healthcare to personal advice, understanding how users rely on them, and when that reliance becomes miscalibrated, grows more urgent. This paper argues that measuring and mitigating overreliance must become central to LLM research and deployment. First, we consolidate risks from overreliance at both the individual and societal levels, including high-stakes errors, governance challenges, and long-term impacts on skill development and autonomy. Then, we explore LLM characteristics, system design features, and user cognitive biases that together raise serious and unique concerns about overreliance on LLMs in practice. We also examine historical approaches for measuring reliance, identifying three gaps and proposing three directions to improve measurement. Finally, we discuss mitigation strategies that can be pursued to ensure LLMs augment rather than diminish human capabilities.
https://doi.org/10.1609/aies.v9i2.43225

[A2] AI Assistance for Human Review of Default Judgments
Theodora Worledge, Othman Bensouda Koraichi [...] Tatsunori Hashimoto* [...] David Freeman Engstrom
Proceedings of the AAAI/ACM Conference on AI Ethics and Society · 11 October 2026
In short: Controlled study finds an LLM-based Default Assistant makes law students 6% more accurate and 26% faster at reviewing default judgments.
Overwhelmed courts in the United States review millions of default judgments each year. Unfortunately, such manual reviews are time-consuming and prone to error. In an audit of 188 debt collection cases granted default judgment by the Superior Court of Los Angeles, we find that 4% contained major defects that should have entirely prevented default judgment, 10% contained inconsistencies requiring reduced judgments, and 32% contained errors requiring amendment prior to judgment. To support courthouses in default judgment review, we collaborated with courthouse attorneys and judges in designing a Default Assistant. The Default Assistant employs large language models to evaluate a case with respect to predetermined legal requirements and provide cited recommendations for an expert user's review. We equip users to verify these recommendations by grounding the assistant's explanations in cited quotes and tables from the original case filings. We conduct a controlled study with 66 law students that conservatively simulates court review, with more time and resources than court staff. We nevertheless find users aided by the Default Assistant were 6.0% more accurate on the average requirement than unaided reviewers (p < 1.0e-4). Simultaneously, users were 25.9% faster in reviewing the average requirement than unaided reviewers (p < 2.5e-10). Statutory requirements demanding extensive document search realized the largest gains, with error reductions and time savings from AI assistance up to 62% and 34%, respectively, relative to unassisted user performance and with differences statistically significant (p < 0.05). Our work provides a proof-of-concept that AI assistants with citations have the potential to help resource-constrained courts conduct default judgment review more accurately and efficiently.
https://doi.org/10.1609/aies.v9i4.43347

[B2] Computational propaganda campaigns by domestic political actors in Pakistan on Facebook
Hassan Mustafa, Markus Luczak–Roesch, David Johnstone
Journal of Computational Social Science · 14 October 2026
In short: Facebook hashtag campaigns in Pakistan show bots and spam accounts dominate political propaganda, intensifying around elections.
Abstract Computational propaganda campaigns came into the limelight after the 2016 US elections and since then the research has predominately focused on foreign influence operations across the US, UK, and EU. Computational propaganda campaigns in the Global South remain understudied. Therefore, we shift our focus towards Pakistan and examine computational propaganda campaigns by domestic political actors. Utilizing Facebook digital trace data, we track 400 hashtag campaigns by domestic political actors to analyze: (1) the dissemination patterns of political information, (2) the role bot and spam accounts play in the dissemination of political information, (3) the nature of hashtag campaigns, and (4) the key initiators and amplifiers during the hashtag campaigns. Our research contributions include: (1) we validate the findings in the previous literature regarding presence of bots and increased coordinated activity during the emergence of political events and show that the most central bridging positions within these campaigns are held by organized spam accounts. (2) Our analysis of multi-lingual hashtag campaigns uncovers that language and political context play a pivot role in dissemination of political information. (3) Tracing campaigns across several years, we find that automated and inauthentic activity intensifies around electoral contests, rising to roughly two-fifths of monthly posts by the 2024 general election, when spam accounts came to outnumber verified institutional actors. Together, these findings characterize computational propaganda in Pakistan as a domestically driven, linguistically stratified, and as an issue/event-driven phenomenon.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s42001-026-00502-2

[B3] [Editor's pick] AI-generated language remains systematically distinguishable from human communication
Nicolò Pagan, Petter Törnberg, Christopher A. Bail, Christopher Barrie
PNAS · 12 October 2026
In short: CHAT framework shows LLM outputs stay distinguishable from human social media text, especially in affective tone, even after calibration.
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in the social sciences to simulate human behavior and communication, yet validation methods for these applications remain limited. Existing evaluations often rely on human judgments of plausibility, a coarse, difficult-to-scale, and often unreliable benchmark for distinguishing AI-generated from human text. As a result, researchers lack robust tools for assessing the realism of LLM-generated language and for calibrating models to real-world data. We address this gap in two ways. First, we introduce the Conversational Human Authenticity Test (CHAT), a validation framework that combines aggregate metrics of detectability and semantic similarity with interpretable linguistic features, including stylistic markers and topical patterns, to assess how closely LLMs approximate human language in a given dataset. Second, we benchmark nine open-weight LLMs across five calibration strategies, including fine-tuning, stylistic prompting, and context retrieval, on user interactions from X, Bluesky, and Reddit. We find that even after calibration, LLM outputs remain clearly distinguishable from human text, especially in affective tone and emotional expression. Instruction-tuned models generally underperform base models, and larger models do not obviously improve human-likeness. We also identify a trade-off between realism and semantic fidelity: methods that make outputs seem more human often move them further from the content of human responses. Together, these results provide a scalable framework for validation and calibration in LLM-based social simulation while underscoring current limits in reproducing human communication.
https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2531748123

[B4] Estimating the threat of AI-agent responding across online survey platforms
Grace Zhang, Robert Walatka [...] Craig R. Fox
PNAS · 15 October 2026
In short: Finds 6 to 41% of online survey respondents fail AI-agent tests, showing AI contamination of online survey data varies by platform.
Recent research and advances in LLMs have led to widespread concern that AI agents could pose as human online survey-takers. However, it remains unclear how prevalent AI agents are on survey research platforms and how to effectively detect AI agents. We validated a series of AI detection tests that effectively separated verified-human participants from both prompted commercial and purpose-built AI agents. Using these tests, we collected surveys on seven online platforms and find wide variation in rates of respondents failing AI tests, ranging from 6 to 41% across platforms (compared to a 2.4% false-positive rate for in-person human participants). We further demonstrate that undetected AI agents can impact the results of online surveys. Our findings suggest that while there is an urgent need for AI detection tests and consistent, systematic monitoring of data quality on online platforms, some platforms currently appear to provide data with a low rate of AI agents.
https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2606460123

[B5] Reproducibility of social science research using aggregate statistics with noise infused for differential privacy
Ryan Steed, Eduardo Schnadower Mustri, Alessandro Acquisti
PNAS · 9 October 2026
In short: About 91% of 93 social science regression findings still replicate under typical differential privacy noise levels.
Privacy-preserving analytics such as differential privacy are designed to allow the analysis of sensitive datasets while protecting individuals' privacy. Their deployment, however, has been controversial. Critics maintain that statistical noise injected to preserve privacy can degrade the quality and feasibility of social science research. We select a benchmark of empirical findings from 93 published social science studies involving regression analyses over aggregate statistics. We evaluate whether their findings replicate on privacy noise-infused data. Under privacy budgets typical in industry, around 91% of simulated findings still support the original claims at significance level [Formula: see text]. Claims based on weaker original effect sizes are more likely to be nullified or sometimes reversed. We compare distortions caused by privacy noise to those due to measurement errors and other kinds of nonsampling errors common in social statistics, and we find that the marginal impacts of privacy protection are smaller. Moreover, discrepancies due to privacy noise are often much smaller than discrepancies observed in traditional replication and robustness studies.
https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2605550123

[C1] [Editor's pick] Level up, ladies: Femininity as a total project and the rise of Queen feminism
Heini Salminen, Juulia Heikkinen
Feminist Media Studies · 9 October 2026
In short: Finnish queen influencers construct femininity as a total project, articulating a neoliberal Queen feminism on Instagram.
Critiques of liberal feminisms are increasingly emerging from the women-led “femosphere,” calling for a revised understanding of feminism through a revaluation of femininity. This article examines one such voice: a group of social media influencers who cultivate and commercialize “queen” femininity as a lifestyle, advising women on how to “level up” their lives and attract provider-minded men. We follow 14 Finnish “queen” influencer–entrepreneurs and ask how they define and practice femininity. Drawing on digital fieldwork and content analysis of 303 Instagram captions, we show how femininity is constructed as a total and infinite project—one that extends across all fronts of life. The study further demonstrates that the cultivation of a “feminine lifestyle” is not merely a set of personal preferences but also implies a broader vision of social order. We term this ideological project “Queen feminism”: an essentialized, spiritualized, and platformed articulation of neoliberal feminism, in which the entrepreneurial ethos is (re)located in the total, perpetual, and crucially impossible project of the feminine self. Beyond analyzing a (counter)movement within feminism, this paper contributes to discussions on influencer politics by showing how the entanglement of ideological work and influencer economics may hamper collective feminist projects yet contribute to wider ideological shifts.
https://doi.org/10.1080/14680777.2026.2745988

[C2] [Editor's pick] Hearts for hating Activists: how far-right actors dominate polarized TikTok debates on climate protest
Hendrik Meyer
Information Communication & Society · 11 October 2026
In short: German politicians' TikTok climate protest debates are polarized, with far-right actors dominating content and benefiting from antagonistic engagement.
This study investigates degrees of polarization in political climate protest debates on TikTok. Drawing on a dataset of automatically transcribed TikTok videos posted by German politicians between 2022 and 2025 (N ≈ 1,700), alongside associated user comments (N ≈ 53,000), this study compares discussions on general climate activism, reformist activism (Fridays for Future), and disruptive activism (Letzte Generation). Using a mixed-method approach that combines Large Language Model-based frame classification, stance detection, and network analysis, this study operationalizes Discursive Polarization across ideological, affective, and interactional dimensions. Findings reveal that while all protest debates on TikTok are polarized, disruptive protests are associated with particularly intense attacks and less attention to policy-oriented climate discussions. However, conservative and far-right politicians consistently frame all activist groups as extremists, criminals, or threats to public order, with far-right accounts dominating content production and audience support. Network analyses demonstrate that far-right politicians and their supporters form highly connected clusters in which both emotionally positive and hostile user engagement amplifies elite-level antagonism. Even moderate posts by center-left or progressive politicians attract disproportionate backlash, suggesting asymmetric polarization dynamics linked to partisan user networks. Overall, the study shows that political TikTok debates around climate protest are consistently polarized along ideological lines, but that greater disruptiveness coincides with more intense antagonistic engagement and weaker Connective Action among supporters. Most crucially, far-right politicians emerge as the clear beneficiaries of this polarization, occupying central positions in antagonist networks of Connective Counteraction and dominating the debate through disruption.
https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118x.2026.2737399

[C3] (In)congruent synergies? Discursive boundary-spanning between the Italian anti-gender galaxy and anti-trans groups
Elena Pavan, Aurora Perego, Matteo Scianna
Information Communication & Society · 12 October 2026
In short: Mixed-methods study of Italian social media shows anti-gender and anti-trans actors converge discursively around shared frames, though the alliance is fragile.
This article investigates increasing alignment between the anti-gender galaxy and anti-trans groups by looking at the symbolic synergies that these two collective endeavors create in and through social media discourse. Building on social movement theory, we examine processes of discursive boundary spanning through which ecclesial and right-wing actors, dedicated anti-gender organizations, and anti-trans groups converge when discussing gender-related issues at large on social media, aligning around similar agendas, objects of interest, priorities, and frames, while setting aside differences that might hinder their collaboration. Empirically, the study relies on a mixed-method analysis of Facebook and Instagram posts published between 2021 and 2023 by twenty-two prominent actors mobilizing around anti-gender and anti-trans claims in Italy. Combining topic modeling with a computational adaptation of frame semantic grammar and qualitative frame analysis, we examine patterns of discursive convergence across the four sectors as well as the ideological differences that are bracketed to sustain it. Our findings reveal the existence of a transversal and yet fragile cross-sectoral discursive convergence. While all four sectors align around a limited set of shared interests and priorities, stronger discursive commonalities are found between anti-trans actors, dedicated anti-gender organizations and right-wing actors. Moreover, although common diagnostic frames emphasize the protection of women and children from gender ideology, divergent prognostic frames and long-term horizons persist within and across sectors.
https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118x.2026.2743936

[D1] From a River in Gilead to the Inference Distributions of Large Language Models: Covert Dialect Bias and Linguistic Profiling at Scale
Chowdhury Mohammad Abdullah, Rita Orji
Proceedings of the AAAI/ACM Conference on AI Ethics and Society · 11 October 2026
In short: LLMs show covert dialect bias via probability distributions, penalizing AAVE and Nigerian Pidgin in housing judgments, with context-dependent patterns for Nigerian Standard English.
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being deployed in services that shape consequential life outcomes, from housing platforms to automated screening workflows. While alignment techniques have reduced the explicit expression of racial bias in model outputs, they leave untouched the attitudinal associations reflected in a model's internal probability distributions. These covert biases are invisible to users and resistant to conventional auditing that inspects generated text alone. In this paper, we build upon the matched-guise sociolinguistic paradigm to examine the covert dialect bias of LLMs in housing-related social judgments across four English varieties: Standard American English (SAE), African American Vernacular English (AAVE), Nigerian Standard English (NSE), and Nigerian Pidgin (NP). AAVE reflects the racialized dialect examined in prior covert-bias evaluations, while NSE and NP represent Black African, postcolonial varieties absent from this line of research. Using 260 meaning-matched sentence quadruples and log-probability scoring over housing-relevant adjectives, we probe ten open-weight LLMs across three evaluation contexts that gradually vary in social proximity: tenant screening, neighbor acceptance, and roommate selection. Our results show that AAVE and NP are consistently associated with more negative housing-relevant adjectives than SAE across all ten models, with NP receiving the most severe penalization overall, and that each dialect is penalized through a distinct stereotype cluster rather than as a generic non-standard category. NSE, which shares the Nigerian identity of NP but carries institutional prestige, exhibits a context-dependent pattern that diverges sharply from both: favored over SAE in formal tenant evaluation but penalized as social proximity increases. Together, these results show that LLMs inherit covert dialect bias along two distinct dimensions, racial identity and prestige, echoing housing discrimination long documented among humans and extending its reach beyond the African American community to racialized postcolonial English varieties.
https://doi.org/10.1609/aies.v9i1.43122

[D2] Polarization and echo chambers in Reddit’s political discourse
Daniele Cirulli, Antonio Desiderio, Giulio Cimini, Fabio Saracco
EPJ Data Science · 12 October 2026
In short: Reddit's 2016 election discourse shows clear ideological polarization and echo chambers, with election periods intensifying cross-group engagement and reducing linguistic diversity.
Abstract Political debate nowadays takes place mainly on online social media, with election periods amplifying ideological engagement. Reddit is generally considered more resistant to polarization and echo chamber effects than platforms like Twitter or Facebook. Here, we challenge this assumption through a case study across the 2016 US presidential election.We use statistical validation techniques to extract ideologically distinct communities of subreddits, in terms of their contributing user base and news consumption, which we use to analyze the dynamics of political debate. [D3] Quantifying public opinion evolution and information behavior dynamics: a framework coupling event knowledge graphs with centroid-aligned topic modeling
Juan Wang, Xianghui Kong, Xiaojing Zhang
EPJ Data Science · 9 October 2026
In short: A BERTopic-based framework with event knowledge graphs tracks Weibo opinion evolution and identifies five user profiles during a Chikungunya outbreak.
Public health emergency monitoring on social media is crucial for proactive risk mitigation. However, this process remains constrained by short-text semantic noise and the fragmented integration of discourse structures and behavioral heterogeneity. To bridge this gap, this paper introduces a dual-level framework integrating Event Knowledge Graphs (EKG) and user behavior profiling, built on a Centroid-Alignment Factor (C-AF) embedding-enhanced BERTopic model. Applied to 22,744 Weibo posts related to the Chikungunya fever outbreak, the C-AF model optimizes Topic Diversity (TD) from 0.5725 to 0.6480, enabling the construction of a high-precision EKG that reveals core discourse evolutionary pathways. For user behavior segmentation, the framework achieves a peak Silhouette Coefficient of 0.909 (outperforming the 0.670 baseline by 35.7%), further validated by an external semantic indicator (TC improving from 0.550 to 0.783). This dual-validation successfully identifies five distinct user profiles: Early Cognitive, Knowledge Dissemination, Risk-Attention, Prevention-Focused, and Preventive Alert types. This framework offers a scalable, data-driven tool for precise public health crisis monitoring and targeted digital governance intervention.
https://doi.org/10.1140/epjds/s13688-026-00709-3

[E2] FaciliTrain: Practicing Facilitation Skills through AI-Simulated Group Dialogue
Hang Jiang, Yuanxin Zhu, Diyi Yang* [...] Jad Kabbara
ACM Conference on Computer Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW) · 9 October 2026
In short: Voice-based AI-simulated group dialogue training improved accuracy comparably, but treatment participants' comfort declined while controls' improved, and they preferred AI feedback.
Skilled facilitation supports inclusive small-group dialogue, but deliberate practice is hard to scale: it depends on expert coaches, live practice partners, and iterative feedback. We present FaciliTrain, a voice-based training system in which learners step into the facilitator role of an AI-simulated multi-participant conversation, apply five evidence-based techniques, and receive structured AI feedback to support reflection. We report findings from a mixed-methods study with 24 participants, conducted as a formative study (N = 12) and a controlled pilot (N = 12; 6 treatment, 6 control). Both conditions achieved comparable accuracy on a live evaluation task, though treatment participants' self-rated comfort declined significantly while control participants' comfort improved (p = .018). Reflexive thematic analysis identifies four themes: the taxonomy externalizes implicit facilitation intuitions; Making Connections is the most cognitively demanding technique; voice acts as a deliberate-response forcing function; and participants overwhelmingly preferred AI feedback over self-practice. We discuss design implications for voice-based, AI-supported interpersonal skill training at scale.

[E3] [Preprint] GENDIS: Gender-Related Discourse and Stance in Multilingual YouTube Comments from India, Kenya, and Nigeria
Luise Koch, Manuel Tonneau* [...] Sharath Chandra Guntuku
arXiv (Cornell University) · 13 October 2026
In short: Introduces GENDIS, a multilingual annotated YouTube comment dataset showing gender-related discourse is mostly neutral or positive and lightly supervised classifiers rival LLMs.
Research on gender-related online content is often operationalized through harm-centered tasks that collapse non-harmful gender-related and unrelated content, while existing datasets remain concentrated in high-resource languages and Global North settings. To bridge these gaps, we introduce GENDIS, a dataset of 9,600 multilingual, human-annotated YouTube comments from India, Kenya, and Nigeria (3,200 per country), annotated using a two-level framework that first identifies whether gender is referenced and then classifies gender-related content as negative, positive, or neutral. This separation provides a denominator for gender-related discourse independent of harm, enabling both prevalence estimation and characterization of its evaluative range. In the uniform random stratum, 23.8-35.0% of comments are gender-related, and among those with a resolved stance, 82% are neutral or positive. Across models, gender-reference detection is substantially easier than stance classification, especially for positive and neutral speech. Lightly supervised classifiers match frontier zero-shot large language models at substantially lower cost and outperform generic toxicity detection, while cross-country transfer remains limited. GENDIS provides a locally grounded resource for measuring when gender is invoked online and how that reference is framed.

[E4] [Preprint] Pushback in a Haystack: Language Models Respond to Factual Errors Ambiguously and Ineffectively
Li Lucy, Myra Cheng* [...] Yulia Tsvetkov*
arXiv (Cornell University) · 13 October 2026
In short: LLMs push back on users' false claims indirectly and mix in affirmation, reducing correction effectiveness, with more indirectness in Hindi and Yoruba; a human study confirms users' misconceptions persist.
The sycophancy of language models, or their failure to push back against faulty user input, is now widely studied. However, if LMs do push back, is sycophancy solved? We show that LMs vary in how they present pushback, and thus vary in how effectively they correct user beliefs. We introduce a phrase-level, human-validated response labeling framework to uncover fine-grained insights into how LMs push against and/or affirm false inputs. We develop and apply our framework on model responses in five languages: English, German, Chinese, Hindi, and Yoruba. In a large-scale human study (n = 300), we find that the pushback ambiguity we identified may fail to correct and prevent users' factual misconceptions.
