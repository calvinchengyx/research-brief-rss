A2YouTube Clips and Broad but Shallow Attention to Candidates: Evidence from the 2024 Tokyo Gubernatorial Election
義人 三輪, Kazutoshi Sasahara
arXiv (Cornell University) · 17 October 2026
In short: YouTube clip videos gave Tokyo gubernatorial candidates broad but shallow attention, with clips driving views but fewer comments.
Video-sharing platforms and social media have gained influence in election campaigns, and third-party edited videos (called kirinuki in Japanese; hereafter, "clip videos") in particular have attracted attention. In Japan, Shinji Ishimaru, previously unknown nationally, finished second in the 2024 Tokyo gubernatorial election, a result widely attributed to his popularity on YouTube. Focusing on this case, we analyzed 44,331 YouTube videos relating to the nine leading candidates, posted between July 5, 2020, and July 7, 2024. Using cumulative views and engagement data as of June 25, 2025, we examined the relationship between clip videos and the attention candidates received. Regression analyses with standard errors clustered by channel showed that Ishimaru, the most watched candidate, did not draw the most active engagement. Videos mentioning Ishimaru received about 36% fewer likes and 41% fewer comments at the same number of views, whereas some candidates with fewer views recorded higher engagement rates. Clip videos were associated with this breadth of attention. They received about 1.8 times more views but 26% fewer comments at the same number of views, and mentions of Ishimaru were associated with about 2.6 times more views among non-clip videos. The interaction between the two, however, was not significant, and understanding the prominence of Ishimaru's cumulative views requires considering both the number of clip videos about him and the views per video. These results suggest that the spread of attention through clip videos may have functioned as a broad but shallow pathway, distinct from the formation of committed support.

A3X-pressing Intimacy: Mental Health Disclosure After Twitter Acquisition
Olivia Pal, Yunhao Yuan [...] Koustuv Saha
arXiv (Cornell University) · 21 October 2026
In short: Mental health self-disclosure on Twitter shifted in linguistic style and intimacy after Musk's acquisition, with condition-specific patterns.
Social media platforms provide spaces for individuals to disclose mental health experiences and seek support, yet how these disclosures evolve during major shifts in platform governance remains unclear. We examine mental health self-disclosure surrounding Elon Musk's October 2022 acquisition of Twitter, analyzing approximately 824K tweets across anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder (BD), borderline personality disorder (BPD), and suicidal ideation (SI) or related experiences, alongside 266K comparison tweets. Using Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) and an LLM-based intimacy classifier, we characterize changes in psycholinguistic expression and disclosure intimacy. Following the acquisition, we observe declines in affective and social language, alongside increases in first-person singular pronouns and future-focused expressions. Intimacy patterns vary by condition: anxiety, depression, and BPD show increases in low-intimacy and decreases in moderate-intimacy disclosures, whereas suicide-related posts show more high-intimacy disclosures. However, compared with broader Twitter discourse, only the relative decline in moderate- versus low-intimacy disclosure for BPD remains statistically significant. Our findings underscore the importance of considering both language and intimacy when studying platform transitions, informing the design of online environments that support sensitive self-disclosure.

A4Beyond Better Ideas: How Human-AI Collaboration Shapes Scientific Ideas and Human Perceptions
Qian Chen, Saatvik Pradhan [...] Wenpeng Yin
arXiv (Cornell University) · 22 October 2026
In short: Human-AI scientific ideation shows no quality advantage for AI collaboration, AI judges disagree with humans, and firsthand AI use shifts perceptions.
As AI increasingly becomes a collaborator in scientific research, its impact may extend beyond improving ideas to reshaping how they are evaluated, attributed, and perceived by humans. Yet existing research has largely examined these dimensions in isolation. We present a unified empirical study of Human-AI scientific ideation, investigating two complementary perspectives: i) how AI collaboration shapes scientific ideas and their evaluation, and ii) how firsthand collaboration reshapes human perceptions of AI. Through a shared experimental setting involving Human-AI and Human-Human research teams, human and LLM evaluators, collaboration-provenance analysis, and participant surveys, we uncover several counterintuitive findings. First, AI collaboration shows no significant advantage in human-evaluated idea quality, while iterative revision consistently improves quality. Second, AI judges can approximate human scores yet disagree on idea rankings; notably, an AI judge favors AI-assisted proposals without a corresponding advantage in human evaluations. Third, collaboration provenance remains only partially detectable, revealing substantial overlap between Human-AI and Human-Human scientific outputs. Finally, firsthand AI collaboration is associated with greater perceived usefulness but smaller efficiency gains than expected, suggesting a nuanced recalibration of human expectations. Overall, our findings challenge the notion that AI's value in scientific ideation lies primarily in producing better ideas, revealing instead its broader influence on scientific creation, evaluation, attribution, and human perceptions.

A5AI-Native Academia Should Build a Community Verification Layer
Haotian Zong, Luhao Zhang
arXiv (Cornell University) · 22 October 2026
In short: Argues for a community verification layer that makes researchers' routine checking of published work reusable and credited.
This paper argues that AI-native academia should build a Community Verification Layer: interoperable, policy-governed infrastructure that converts researchers' routine reading and checking into persistent, claim- and version-linked evidence, while routing scarce deeper verification toward uncertain, consequential, and neglected research. AI is lowering the cost of producing plausible research artifacts faster than the cost of accountable evaluation, yet much of the evaluation researchers already perform while reading, citing, implementing, or extending work is never made reusable. We propose a tiered ladder from comprehension notes to claim checks, audits, artifact execution, reproduction, replication, and downstream validation. The layer would elicit judgments before revealing social signals, preserve multidimensional disagreement, privately verify contributors while allowing flexible public disclosure, allocate attention under expertise and coverage constraints, and make verification labor citable, with credit tied to the quality and depth of the evidence. We distinguish this design from prior open and post-publication review and outline governance requirements and potential counterarguments.

A6Major Revision, Minor Accountability: LLMs as Reviewers of Published Articles in Top-Tier Journals
Bastien Le Guellec, Su Hwan Kim, Raphaël Bentegeac, Aghilès Hamroun
arXiv (Cornell University) · 22 October 2026
In short: LLM peer reviewers of top-journal articles recommend revision or rejection for most papers, with inconsistent verdicts across models and weak flaw detection.
With the growth of scientific output, the peer review system is approaching a breaking point: reviewers are increasingly hard to secure, and turnaround times lengthen accordingly. LLM-assisted or LLM-generated peer review has been proposed as a remedy, but how such models would treat manuscripts already published in top-tier journals, the closest thing the current system has to a gold standard of scientific validity, is not known. Here we assembled a corpus of 200 articles, each released under a CC BY 4.0 license and published in 2026, from 20 general-science, life-science, and clinical-medicine journals including Nature, Science, and Cell, and asked four current LLMs (two proprietary, two open-weight) to produce independent peer-review reports of each one under a blinded protocol. Their decisions were overwhelmingly harsh and starkly inconsistent with each other: 605 of 800 reviews (75.6%) recommended major revision or rejection, but the rate at which any single model did so ranged from 17.5% to 100% (p < 0.001), disagreement on manuscripts that had, by construction, already survived real editorial review at the journals in question. When the same manuscripts were doctored with one deliberately planted, field-specific methodological or statistical flaw, detection rates varied from 17.0% to 60.5% across models (p < 0.001). Recommending revision for papers that had already passed editorial review (low specificity) while missing flaws deliberately placed in front of them (low sensitivity), these models produce output that should be read for what it is: an uneven, framing- and model-dependent signal.

A8S$^2$-MAS: A Society-Scale Multi-Agent Simulation Framework with 500 Million Household Persona Agents
Yang Li, Di Wan [...] Ye Luo
arXiv (Cornell University) · 20 October 2026
In short: Builds a society-scale LLM agent simulation of 500 million household personas to model consumption, debt and credit responses to financial choices.
Simulating economic responses at society scale requires agents whose decisions reflect household circumstances and the effects of earlier actions. We introduce S$^2$-MAS, a society-scale multi-agent simulation framework built on 500 million synthetic household personas grounded in real-survey data. Its Genese engine separates decisions from state updates, while reusable financial scenarios and application ledgers track consumption, cash, debt and payments. Trained with SFT + RLCD on 2.93 million examples, sharp-421m-mlx achieves 74.55% accuracy and 38.37% question-macro F1 on one-shot consumer financial-choice tasks derived from held-out survey responses. Its accuracy exceeds GLM-5.3-Flash, Jev 1.13, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, GPT-6-Luna and GPT-6-Astra in the common benchmark (full test for the first four; matched 500-group sample for Astra). We evaluate eight financial task families and simulate 12 million distinct household agents in paired purchase-funding conditions. Jointly raising purchase goals and approved credit limits increases consumption, with responses differing across age and income groups. Education, employment and household-size differences persist after age-income standardization and mainly reflect transitions from deferral to purchase. Asset and debt groups differ in cash drawdown, repayment and closing debt. A response-stratified 24-month panel varies purchase goals under a common credit product, with repayment and arrears updating subsequent offers. S$^2$-MAS links heterogeneous household decisions to evolving consumption, cash and repayment paths, supporting economic experiments across household groups, population scales and time horizons.

A9Truthful by Omission: Click Thinning for LLM-Native Advertising
Junhua Liu, Zhicheng Liang [...] Feng Qian
arXiv (Cornell University) · 22 October 2026
In short: Proposes a truthful, efficient auction mechanism for sponsored ads embedded in generative AI answers, using random click thinning.
Generative AI assistants are becoming an advertising medium, embedding sponsored content in their answers. This setting departs from classical position auctions in two ways: an ad's click-through rate depends on the passage that hosts it, and because users stop at their first click, earlier ads take clicks from later ones. This competition for clicks makes truthfulness delicate: a higher bid can reshape the allocation and put a competitor ahead of the bidder, reducing the bidder's clicks. Existing mechanisms either attain a constant fraction of the optimal welfare (advertisers' total click value) but are not known to be truthful, or are truthful but their welfare guarantee degrades as the number of passages grows. We achieve both even when the answer and its editorial order are fixed before bidding. Assuming each ad's click-through rate when reached is known and unaffected by omitting other ads, we give a computationally efficient mechanism that is truthful in expectation and attains at least half the optimal welfare under free reordering. Our key idea, click thinning, omits ads at random, showing earlier ads less often so that each ad's overall click probability no longer depends on the order of the ads. Welfare maximization then reduces to a tractable matching problem, and solving it exactly with VCG-style payments makes truthful bidding optimal in expectation. Characterizing the click probabilities that omission can achieve sharpens the approximation factor from $2$ to $2-(1-δ)^{K-1}$ when at most $K$ ads are shown and each click-through rate is at most $δ$. This factor is the best possible for any order-preserving rule, even one that ignores incentives and computation, so truthfulness and computational efficiency incur no additional worst-case loss.

A11Replication Package for "What Ethereum Developers Ask For, and Whether They Get It: Ten Years of Ethereum Stack Exchange"
Tamer Abdelaziz
arXiv (Cornell University) · 22 October 2026
In short: Ten years of Ethereum Stack Exchange show declining answer and acceptance rates, largely driven by newer askers rather than topic mix.
The Ethereum community learns where developers struggle through surveys and interviews, which record what developers say when asked about a chosen theme. Ethereum Stack Exchange (ESE) records what they ask for unprompted, and whether anyone resolves it. We study every question posted to ESE from Jan 2016 to Dec 2025, 54,637 in all, and label each with a prompt-based topic pipeline seeded with eight Ethereum-specific topics and open to new ones (22 topics in all). A three-provider committee of language models screens the labels, and manual adjudication of every disputed case puts their accuracy at 87.9 to 93.5%. Because the corpus is the complete record, no rate carries sampling error. We ask what developers ask for help with, whether the venue still resolves it, and, where it does not, whether the questions changed or their resolution did. Demand is concentrated on writing, deploying, and debugging contracts; the toolchain is the second-largest topic and growing, while security, consensus, and scaling are thin. Supply has weakened, and unevenly: answers still arrive, closure falls further. Across five estimators of the decade, the share of questions drawing any answer fell by 9.5 to 18.6 percentage points, but among answered questions the share reaching an accepted answer fell by 16.2 to 24.5. Rebuilding the later acceptance rate at the earlier topic mix moves the fall only from 9.40 to 9.32 points, so the topic mix does not explain it. Who asks separates outcomes far more sharply than what is asked: acceptance runs from 19.8% for askers below 10 reputation to 66.8% at 10k and above, a 47- point spread with a gradient in every year, against 17 across topics, and a shift toward newer askers accounts for up to 59% of the acceptance fall. The largest of seven properties an asker controls accompanies 5 points. The record places the support gap in the toolchain and among newcomers.

A12What the submission does not show: Complexifying cognitive offloading in students' mathematical work with LLMs
Igor’ Kontorovich, Miriam N Wallach
arXiv (Cornell University) · 22 October 2026
In short: Study of 23 students finds LLM-aided submissions are not reliable markers of cognitive offloading in math homework.
A common concern is that using large language models (LLMs) lets students offload mathematical work that tasks are designed to elicit. Yet such claims often rest on outcomes (e.g., submitted solutions), rather than on evidence of students' work with LLMs. We followed 23 first-year linear algebra students as they solved three differently designed homework tasks with LLMs. Drawing on self-regulated learning and choice-affluent environments, we interpreted each of 429 prompts as a trace of a regulatory event and compared 59 chatlogs with the problem solutions the students submitted. Prompts formed five structures, casting the LLM mainly as a solver, explainer, or validator. The mix of structures shifted across the tasks, and ten of the 15 students who completed all three tasks changed their dominant structure. Most final submissions stayed close to LLM output, including those that followed exchanges in which students had explained, conjectured, and checked their own ideas. We conclude that an LLM-aided submission is not a trustworthy marker of students' offloading of the mathematical work, and that offloading needs to be examined in each regulatory space and part by part rather than as a whole.

A13A Grammar of French Civil Cassation: A Ground-Level Dataset and Tool
Avrile Floro, Tamara Dhorasoo [...] Maria Boritchev
arXiv (Cornell University) · 22 October 2026
In short: Releases a dataset of French Court of Cassation rulings paired with grounds and reasons, revealing how civil appeal outcomes evolve over a decade.
This paper presents a dataset of 121,536 ground-reasons pairs across 86,464 rulings of the five civil chambers of the French Court of Cassation (2016-2025). An appeal on points of law cannot itself be appealed and often decides a case for good. Understanding how the Court rules is thus crucial and can help lawyers who draft those appeals. Inside a ruling, every ground of appeal (a distinct legal complaint against the appealed decision) has its own answer from the Court. This answer is called the reasons and it belongs to one of a few doctrinal families. Using a deterministic pipeline, we pair each ground with its reasons and attach the pair to its family, according to the established French taxonomy. We obtain a descriptive grammar of civil cassation that shows which grounds succeed, which legal texts they refer to, and how both evolve over the decade. We release the dataset, the code and an interactive consultation tool.

C1Data-Void Exploits in Web-Searching LLMs
Michele Guerra, Wuyeh Jobe [...] Azza Abouzied
arXiv (Cornell University) · 21 October 2026
In short: Shows disinformers can exploit data voids to get a single planted page cited in web-searching LLM answers 31% of the time, with user prompt tone offering limited protection.
Web-searching Large Language Models (LLMs) have changed how users seek online information: instead of sifting through ranked lists of links, users read synthesized answers. This shift creates a new LLM vulnerability: a data-void exploit. Disinformers publish their narratives in search spaces where relevant and reliable content is scarce, i.e. data voids, then rely on the LLM's web-search to retrieve and then use, cite, or list those pages in its responses to data-void seeking prompts. Unlike classical search, where users see ranking cues and competing results, LLMs can obscure when an answer relies on sparse, unverified, or adversarial sources. We study this threat by varying the user's prompt tone - skeptical, excited, or neutral - and LLM choice - ChatGPT, Gemini, or Sonar Pro - as well as three attacker controls. The attacker can use one of five known strategies to construct and fill a data void with their narrative, host their content at varying levels of visibility from well-established, indexed domains to less established ones, and include generative-optimization engine features thought to boost citing by web-searching LLMs. We find that data-void exploits can succeed with only a single attacker controlled page. Across all conditions, attacker-content appears in LLM responses 31% of the time. User controls offer limited protection: while skeptical prompts slightly reduce success and slightly increase flagging or warnings in LLM responses, we find no evidence of large effects of prompt tone or model choice on success rates, although smaller effects cannot be ruled out. Overall, our results establish data voids as a practical inference-time attack surface for manipulating web-searching LLMs through strategically placed web content and highlight the urgent need for defenses against such exploits.

C2In-Browser Human-AI Co-Writing for Counterspeech: An Exploratory User Study of Agency, Perceived Response Quality, and Time
Tammy Schmidt, Luise Koch
arXiv (Cornell University) · 22 October 2026
In short: An LLM co-writing tool helped users write counterspeech with higher peer-rated empathy and persuasiveness, but took longer and conflicted with users' desire to express anger.
Writing counterspeech that challenges online hate speech can be cognitively and emotionally demanding, whereas fully automated generation may weaken users' sense of voice and responsibility. We present an in-browser AI-assisted co-writing tool that enables users to contribute their own ideas and offers three editable suggestions generated by a large language model using dynamically retrieved hate speech-counterspeech examples from the Multitarget CONAN dataset. We evaluated the tool in an exploratory within-subject study with ten participants, complemented by think-aloud observations and semi-structured interviews with half of the participants. Participants wrote responses to three self-selected hate speech instances without assistance and then repeated the task with the tool. Final responses were subsequently peer-rated for empathy, non-toxicity, relevance, specificity, and persuasiveness. Participants evaluated the interface positively, and qualitative accounts linked user input, selection among alternatives, and small edits to perceived agency and responsibility. Assisted responses received higher descriptive mean ratings across all five proxies, with the largest difference for empathy. Mean composition time increased from 2.52 to 3.88 minutes. Qualitative findings further revealed a tension between empathy-oriented outputs and users' desire to express legitimate anger or direct disagreement. We contribute exploratory design guidance for treating AI-assisted counterspeech as deliberative co-writing rather than automated text production.

C3Invisibilization as a Source of Probability Bias in Language Models
Christian Arnold, Ernesto F. Calvo [...] Tiago Ventura
arXiv (Cornell University) · 22 October 2026
In short: LLMs decline to assess reporters described as Black far more often than white reporters, creating a racial probability bias in trust judgments that standard audits miss.
Language models are aligned to avoid using protected attributes such as race, ethnicity, and gender to inform their responses. Therefore, they increasingly refuse or hedge when such attributes appear in a prompt. However, if a system must decide how much to learn from what people tell it, it is costly to refuse valuable information because of its source. In this article, we measure the cost of declining information in a two-urn game with an exact Bayesian answer. We present leading LLM models from two developers (OpenAI and Anthropic) with a prompt that requires them to decide how far to trust an unverifiable report from a participant whose description we randomize across ten configurations (6,600 sessions). We show that leading models often decline to assess the reporter, treating information as worthless at rates set by the description rather than by the evidence. At its widest, models decline a Black-described reporter 46% of the time and a white-described reporter 9%; yet, when they do assess the information, the Black-described reporter receives slightly more trust. The bias is invisible to audits that compare average trust or look only at assessed cases. It grows with reasoning effort, follows words rather than categories (models invoke race for "Black" but not for its Spanish translation negro, and never for "Indigenous"), and worsens under warning instructions a practitioner would try first. We also present a scoring option that reduces the refusal rate bias. When a model's trust is scored against revealed truth over repeated rounds, it credits identical records equally. In four of five configurations from Anthropic and OpenAI, the racial gap in trust falls below 0.02 by the last rounds.
